- widely used query and response protocol designed to access database that stores information about registered internet resources
- Primarily associated with domain names, WHOIS can also provide details about IP address blocks and autonomous systems

- `whois` typically contains following information
-  `Domain Name`: The domain name itself (e.g., example.com)
- `Registrar`: The company where the domain was registered (e.g., GoDaddy, Namecheap)
- `Registrant Contact`: The person or organization that registered the domain.
- `Administrative Contact`: The person responsible for managing the domain.
- `Technical Contact`: The person handling technical issues related to the domain.
- `Creation and Expiration Dates`: When the domain was registered and when it's set to expire.
- `Name Servers`: Servers that translate the domain name into an IP address.


## History

- WHOIS was created in the 1970s by Elizabeth Feinler and her team at the Stanford Research Institute's Network Information Center (NIC).
- It was originally designed to track and manage network users, hostnames, and domain names on ARPANET.
- WHOIS was formally standardized in 1982 through RFC 812.
- Ken Harrenstien and Vic White helped define the WHOIS query-response protocol.
- As the internet grew, the centralized WHOIS database became difficult to manage efficiently.
- During the 1990s, management was distributed among Regional Internet Registries (RIRs).
- Randy Bush and John Postel contributed to the development of the distributed RIR system.
- The distributed model improved scalability and allowed WHOIS to support the rapidly expanding internet.
- In 1998, ICANN was established to oversee DNS management and WHOIS policies.
- Vint Cerf played a key role in establishing ICANN.
- ICANN standardized WHOIS data formats and improved domain management processes.
- ICANN introduced the Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy (UDRP) to resolve domain ownership and trademark disputes.
- Public WHOIS records traditionally exposed information such as names, addresses, phone numbers, and email addresses.
- Privacy concerns led to the development of domain privacy and WHOIS protection services.
- The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), implemented in 2018, significantly restricted public access to personal WHOIS data.
- WHOIS operators were required to comply with stricter data protection and privacy regulations.
- Modern WHOIS focuses on balancing transparency with user privacy.
- The Registration Data Access Protocol (RDAP) is being adopted as a modern alternative to WHOIS.
- RDAP provides structured responses, better authentication mechanisms, and stronger privacy controls.
- WHOIS remains an important tool for domain investigations, cybersecurity research, network administration, and incident response.


### Why WHOIS matter?
- WHOIS data serves as a treasure trove of information for penetration testers during the reconnaissance phase of an assessment
- offers valuable insights into the target organisation's digital footprint and potential vulnerabilities:

- `Identifying Key Personnel`: WHOIS records often reveal the names, email addresses, and phone numbers of individuals responsible for managing the domain. This information can be leveraged for social engineering attacks or to identify potential targets for phishing campaigns.
- `Discovering Network Infrastructure`: Technical details like name servers and IP addresses provide clues about the target's network infrastructure. This can help penetration testers identify potential entry points or misconfigurations.
- `Historical Data Analysis`: Accessing historical WHOIS records through services like [WhoisFreaks](https://whoisfreaks.com/) can reveal changes in ownership, contact information, or technical details over time. This can be useful for tracking the evolution of the target's digital presence.
